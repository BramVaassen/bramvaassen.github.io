Research

Most of my research focuses on causation: what it is and how what it is shapes or constrains arguments in philosophy.

I have published work on why causal arguments for physicalism are not as good as they seem, on why a naive semantics ‘therefore’ is better than it seems, and on why treating chatbots as humans is exactly as undignified as it seems.

Published work

Open-access versions are linked via the title.

2026

Dualist Approaches to Mental Causation
Philosophy Compass: e70109 · https://doi.org/10.1111/phc3.70109

A survey of recent strategies dualists use to defend mental causation against the exclusion problem.

Abstract

Dualism is often thought to rule out mind-body causal interaction, since the physical world already seems causally self-sufficient on its own. This review essay surveys recent attempts to resist that conclusion, sorting them into views that deny physical self-sufficiency and views that accept it, and argues that accepting physical self-sufficiency is the more promising route for dualists to take.

2026

Narrative Therefore
The Philosophical Quarterly · doi.org/10.1093/pq/pqag011

Argues that everyday narrative uses of 'therefore' show the word is ambiguous, against the standard Gricean picture.

Abstract

'Therefore' can be used to narrate how one event led to another, not just to draw a logical inference. This paper argues that such narrative uses put pressure on the classic Gricean account of what 'therefore' contributes to a sentence's meaning, rejects two attempts to rescue that account, and proposes that 'therefore' is better understood as having more than one distinct use.

2026

AI Mimicry and Human Dignity: Chatbot Use as a Violation of Self-Respect

 with Jan-Willem van der Rijt and Dimitri Coelho Mollo
Journal of Applied Philosophy 43 (1): 95–111 · doi: 10.1111/japp.70037

Argues that treating chatbots the way we treat people is a subtle violation of our own self-respect.

Abstract

Chatbots mimic human conversation convincingly enough that people tend to treat them as though they were moral agents, despite lacking the capacities that would make that appropriate. Drawing on a relational account of dignity, the paper argues that this kind of interaction undermines the user's own self-respect, illustrating the point through everyday chatbot uses such as customer service, advice, and companionship.

2024

Against Causal Arguments in Metaphysics
Asian Journal of Philosophy 3 (2): 1–13 · doi.org/10.1007/s44204-024-00184-5

Contends that causal arguments, old and new, don't actually settle debates about physicalism.

Abstract

Arguments that appeal to causal facts have long been used to defend physicalism about the mind, and a newer version aims for a more modest conclusion while avoiding the flaws of the old one. This paper argues that neither the traditional nor the new causal argument succeeds, because causal considerations simply don't run deep enough to settle the underlying metaphysical dispute.

2024

Mental Causation for Standard Dualists
Australasian Journal of Philosophy 102 (4): 978–998 · doi.org/10.1080/00048402.2024.2335325

Defends dualism against the standard objection that it leaves no causal work for the mind to do.

Abstract

Dualists are usually thought to face a problem: if all behaviour already has sufficient physical causes, there's no room left for the mind to do any causal work, and some argue dualists must revise their picture of the mind to fix this. Drawing on recent work in the philosophy of causation, this paper argues that no such revision is needed, and that ordinary dualist views can accommodate mental causation as they already stand.

2023

Absence and Abnormality
Analysis 83 (1): 98–106 · doi.org/10.1093/analys/anac030

Uses cases of double prevention to challenge leading 'norm-based' accounts of when absences cause things.

Abstract

Theories of causation struggle with absences: treating them all as causes seems too permissive, while ruling them all out seems too restrictive. A popular fix appeals to norms, on which an absence counts as a cause only when its presence would have been the 'normal' preventer. This paper shows that cases involving so-called double-prevention mechanisms generate counterexamples to the two leading versions of this normality-based approach.

2022

Halfway Proportionality
Philosophical Studies 179: 2823–2843 · doi.org/10.1007/s11098-022-01803-8

Argues that only half of the popular 'proportionality principle' for causes actually holds up.

Abstract

The proportionality principle holds that causes should be exactly as strong as needed for their effects — not too little, not too much. Critics reject the whole principle over its counterexamples, while defenders lean on it to resolve the causal exclusion problem. This paper argues both sides are partly right: one half of the principle is too demanding and should be dropped, but the other half survives and still does useful philosophical work.

2022

AI, Opacity, and Personal Autonomy
Philosophy and Technology 35 (4): 1–20 · doi.org/10.1007/s13347-022-00577-5

Argues that opaque AI decision systems can undermine autonomy even when their decisions are accurate.

Abstract

AI systems used in decisions like bail, hiring, or diagnosis are often criticized for being 'opaque' — unable to explain their own outputs. Building on a causal account of explanation, this paper identifies a distinct moral cost of that opacity: it can hide the levers a person could otherwise pull to shape decisions that affect their life, thereby undermining their personal autonomy.

2022

And Therefore

 with Alex Sandgren
Inquiry · doi.org/10.1080/0020174X.2022.2075455

Argues that 'therefore' directly asserts a dependence relation, rather than merely implying or presupposing one.

Abstract

In sentences like 'the switch is on, and therefore the lights are on', it's widely agreed that 'therefore' conveys a dependence between the two facts, but there's disagreement about how. This paper argues that the dependence is asserted as part of the sentence's core content, not merely implied or presupposed, and defends this view against the standard objections using a general theory of linguistic projection.

2021

Causal Exclusion without Causal Sufficiency
Synthese 198: 10341–10353 · doi.org/10.1007/s11229-020-02723-y

Shows that exclusion arguments survive even if their notion of 'causal sufficiency' is dropped.

Abstract

Some critics argue that exclusion arguments against non-reductive physicalism rely on a shaky notion of causal sufficiency. This paper shows the objection doesn't really bite: exclusion arguments can simply be restated in terms of physical sufficiency instead, and while some problems remain for exclusionists, they have nothing to do with this particular choice of terms.

2021

Dualism and Exclusion
Erkenntnis 86: 543–552 · dx.doi.org/10.1007/s10670-019-00118-1

Responds to a challenge from List and Stoljar, defending exclusion arguments against dualist theories of mind.

Abstract

List and Stoljar argued that a standard premise in exclusion arguments against dualism proves too much, since it would also exclude properties that are obviously causal. This paper agrees that their target premise has that flaw, but argues that exclusion arguments against dualism don't actually need it, and proposes a weaker premise that avoids the problem while still doing the argumentative work.

2016

Basic Beliefs and the Perceptual Learning Problem
Episteme 13: 133–149 · doi.org/10.1017/epi.2015.58

Argues that expert perceivers pose a problem moderate foundationalist theories of perception can't easily solve.

Abstract

Moderate foundationalists hold that some beliefs — especially perceptual ones — are justified independently of any other beliefs. This paper argues that expert perceivers, who form more justified basic beliefs than novices thanks to perceptual learning, pose a real challenge here, since existing attempts to characterize the relevant expertise either overgeneralize it to novices too, or fail to secure the independent justification foundationalism requires.

Research projects

During 2026–2029, I am involved in two projects funded by the Swedish Research Council:

Natural Properties — a causation-first approach

with Jenn McDonald and Tom Wysocki

This project aims to uncover what makes properties natural by comparing the causal roles of stereotypically natural and unnatural properties. Our working hypothesis is that naturalness tracks causal role: a property like being a proton or a moose is less natural than being a proton, because it plays a more limited causal role and is less suited to good explanations. The account is reductive, nominalist-friendly, avoids ruling out properties by fiat, and captures the explanatory and predictive value of intuitively natural properties.

How words mean: lessons from large language models

with Jessica Pepp (PI), Dimitri Coelho Mollo, and Pär Sundström

Given the right prompt, ChatGPT will output "ibuprofen can increase the risk of gastric ulcers" — the same string a human doctor might produce. This project asks whether the two outputs really share a meaning. One aim is to test the hypothesis that ChatGPT's output is meaningful but differs in meaning from the doctor's utterance; the other is to develop an account on which the two share a meaning, via "quasi-mental states" that ground the model's outputs without underlying mental states.

Previous projects

Causation, Correlation, and Insensitivity

Most causal relations are insensitive: they persist under natural changes to the causal situation — smoking correlates with lung cancer however the smoking itself is caused. Mere correlation is often sensitive by contrast: the cancer–yellow-teeth correlation breaks down where the yellowing isn't caused by smoking. This project argued that patterns of insensitivity can help distinguish causation from the spurious correlations that challenge state-of-the-art accounts of causation. Funded by a Swedish Research Council international postdoc grant; hosted jointly by Rutgers and Umeå, August 2021 – December 2023.

Determinism, Control, and the Consequence Argument

with Andreas Hüttemann (PI) and Christian Loew

Investigated whether control over our actions is compatible with determinism. The project argued that the apparent tension only arises given further, empirically contestable assumptions about causation, laws, counterfactuals, and modality — including those underlying the Consequence Argument, widely taken to be the strongest case for the incompatibility of control and determinism. Part of the DFG-funded Inductive Metaphysics project, March 2020 – July 2021.

PhD thesis

Causal After All: A Model of Mental Causation for Dualists

Supervision: Pär Sundström (primary), Gunnar Björnsson, Torfinn Huvenes (2016–2019), Andreas Stokke (2014–2016). PhD conferred Fall 2019, Umeå University.

Defends the thesis that a standard dualist ontology of mind can allow for mental causation even where all events have sufficient physical causes — that dualist mental phenomena can non-overdeterministically cause behaviour in worlds where the physical realm is causally complete — and critically assesses previous proposals to this effect.

Shortlisted for the 2020 Mercier Prize for monographs in metaphysics and first philosophy (KU Leuven / Université Catholique de Louvain).

Full text via DiVA; hard copies available on request.
